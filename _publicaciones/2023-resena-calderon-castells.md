    valor: "To write a review of The New Latin America is a challenge, both because of the range of topics it addresses and the fact that the region – like the rest of the world – has undergone enormous transformations in the past two years. One may fairly ask, is the situation portrayed by Calderón and Castells still ‘new’? I think so: despite recent political, economic and health crises, the book provides a comprehensive overview of the region and the challenges it faces today. Indeed, it is difficult to disagree with Calderón and Castells's pessimistic evaluation: ‘under the ruins of all political projects and a barely credible democracy, there remained fragmented societies, in which people could only depend on themselves and occasionally on their families’ (p. 315). The COVID-19 pandemic only seems to have exacerbated these challenges. The authors' accurate diagnosis results from their extensive experience in the region, both individually and as a team. Readers familiar with Castells's work will find common themes from his classic The Information Age (1996). Much like that work, the aim here is to offer an up-to-date and intensive overview of the economy, politics and culture of the region, emphasising more recent changes. The book, divided into twelve chapters, begins by providing an exhaustive analysis of the origins of the crisis of the neoliberal and developmentalist models that defined the last twenty years of economic life in the region. From there, the authors trace the emergence of a new production model, ‘informational extractivism’. Such a model would result from exploring, exploiting and commercialising products such as copper, gas, soybeans or coca through global value chains. The book also examines cultural transformations. On the one hand, it considers transformations of collective identities, such as the gender system, or the decline of patriarchy and of traditional religious identities. On the other hand, it reveals changes in individuation processes, such as young people constructing their identity through social networks. Lastly, Calderón and Castells look at the decline of state legitimacy, highlighting how corruption, together with criminal economies, has resulted in a growing distrust of political participation. This, however, does not imply a hopeless future. On the contrary, the authors highlight the emergence of new social actors such as environmental, feminist and indigenous movements, which offer opportunities for change in the midst of the crises. Furthermore, they show how new communication technologies can strengthen the public space, opening it to ‘alternative voices, diverse cultures and identities, and multiplicity of opinions’ (p. 212). The strength of this analysis lies not in the novelty of the topics covered. Several of the book's arguments draw on previous studies on corruption, social movements, gender or urban development. Nevertheless, here these issues are placed on a large canvas, where scholars can appreciate connections generally overlooked for the sake of specialisation. An additional virtue of the book is its ambitious spatial perspective. This is not a comparative study examining a variable in different settings. It aims to investigate structures and actors to identify the region's specific features. Taking an approach that is unusual nowadays, Calderón and Castells analyse Latin America as a whole, weaving stories ranging from small local communities in Bolivia to large cities in Brazil. In this way, they make a crucial contribution to the rebuilding of a regional agenda. Unfortunately, this broad view tends to fall short in detailing differences between countries or clarifying the relationship between the analysed phenomena. For instance, this occurs with the chapter dedicated to the crisis of the Catholic Church or the discussion on drug trafficking (pp. 86–93). Though they are relevant topics, their dynamics do not quite tie in with the general themes of the book. The absence of classic issues in the existing literature, such as the world of labour, is also odd, something that becomes more evident towards the end of the book when the authors suggest – without developing the idea in detail – that the creativity and solidarity of informal workers can help overcome existing crises (p. 316). Despite these shortcomings – understandable for such an ambitious project – this book will be of great interest not only to sociologists but also to any person interested in acquiring a comprehensive insight into the region's dynamics. Specialists seeking to situate their research topics in broader disciplinary and geographic perspectives will also benefit from it. As initially noted, despite the transformations experienced in the recent past, the reader will find this book highly relevant and timely, as the need to build a sustainable economy, solid democratic institutions and better environmental relations is more critical than ever."
